# HOW TO STORYTELLING

A practical guide for crafting data-based stories in business presentations and written communication.

## 1. Core Principle

**A story is not a decoration around your data—it is the delivery mechanism that makes your audience understand, feel, and remember your message.**

Stories engage three channels simultaneously:

| Channel | Role | How a story delivers it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Logos** (intellect) | Structured argument, facts, data | Story structure gives the audience a predictable frame to process information |
| **Pathos** (emotion) | Feeling, urgency, empathy | Narrative tension, surprise, and personal stakes |
| **Ethos** (credibility) | Trust in the speaker | First-person experience, transparency about struggle |

> Rule of thumb: If your audience can only recall the numbers but not the *why*, you presented data. If they can retell the story, you told a story.

## 2. Before You Start: Three Questions

Answer these before writing a single slide or paragraph.

| Question | Sub-questions |
| --- | --- |
| **Who?** | Who is the audience? How familiar are they with the subject? How much detail do they need? What do you need to establish credibility? |
| **What?** | What do you want them to *know*? What do you want them to *feel*? What do you want them to *do*? |
| **How?** | What structure fits the message? What visuals or backup materials support it? Which data points carry the key points? |

## 3. Working with Data

### Slide design principles

- **Fewer words.** Cut every word that does not advance the message.
- **BLOT headline.** The headline states the conclusion, not the topic. ("Revenue up 12 %" not "Q3 Revenue").
- **Chunk information.** Break dense content into 3–4 distinct visual blocks.
- **One restrained palette.** Avoid multiple font colours on a single slide.
- **Label everything.** Graphs, tables, and images need clear titles and axis labels.
- **Use builds.** Reveal data progressively to control the narrative pace.
- **Use contrast.** Make the key data point visually dominant.

### Common anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Key point buried at the bottom of the slide | Move it to the headline (BLOT) |
| Wall of text with mixed font colours | Chunk into 3–4 blocks; one accent colour max |
| Raw data table with no narrative frame | Add a Situation → Complication → Resolution arc |
| No call to action | End with a concrete next step |

## 4. Story Structures

### 4.1 Executive Summary (short-form)

Use when time is limited or the audience is senior.

```text
HOOK        → Main point, intriguing question, or reason to listen
KEY POINTS  → 3–4 messages in top-down order
CONCLUSION  → Summary or powerful call to action
```

Expand or compress depending on audience needs. The one-line version of your key message is your BLOT.

### 4.2 The Hero's Journey (personal narrative)

The ur-plot. Use for stories about personal or team success, failure, or transformation.

| Stage | What happens | Business use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Life is led | "Once upon a time..." – set the scene | Background: who you are, current state |
| 2. A challenge arises | An unexpected event disrupts the status quo | The problem or opportunity appears |
| 3. The challenge is faced | Confront the obstacle; triumph or defeat | The work, the struggle, the turning point |
| 4. Life is forever changed | The world is different now | Impact, results, new reality |
| 5. *(Optional)* What you learned | Explicit lesson or insight | The takeaway your audience should carry |

> Make each stage distinct. Do not skip the struggle—audiences expect it, and it builds credibility.

### 4.3 BLOT / Situation / Complication / Resolution (business argument)

Adapted from Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle. Use for structured business cases.

| Stage | Purpose | Key rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **BLOT** (Bottom Line On Top) | State your main point, ask, or solution up front | The audience knows what to listen for |
| **Situation** | Essential context and background | Do not overload; only what the next stage requires |
| **Complication** | The obstacle that breaks the status quo | Make it vivid and specific; this generates suspense |
| **Resolution** | The solution in detail | Expand on the BLOT; summarise and wrap up |

After crafting the full story, produce two variants:

- **Three-minute version:** the key message when the audience is short on time.
- **One-line version:** your BLOT in a single sentence.

## 5. The Seven Basic Plots

Based on Christopher Booker's *The Seven Basic Plots*. Use as framing devices to shape events into a coherent narrative.

| # | Plot | Core dynamic | Business example | Key stages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Slay the Beast** | Overcome a hostile external force | Facing a market threat or competitor | Call → Initial success → Confrontation → Final ordeal → Miraculous escape |
| 2 | **Rags to Riches** | Rise from low status to high | Sudden business success | Wretchedness → Initial success → Central crisis → Independence → Final union |
| 3 | **The Quest** | Pursue a distant, difficult goal | New project or initiative | Call → Journey → Arrival & frustration → Final ordeals → Goal |
| 4 | **Voyage and Return** | Journey to a new world, return changed | Discovering a new way of working | Fall into another world → Fascination → Frustration → Nightmare → Thrilling escape & return |
| 5 | **Comedy** | Confusion and misunderstandings resolved | Resolving team misalignment | Confusion → Nightmare → New information transforms the situation |
| 6 | **Tragedy** | Inner flaw leads to downfall | Project fails due to mismanagement | Anticipation → Dream → Frustration → Nightmare → Destruction |
| 7 | **Rebirth** | Emerge from darkness into a new life | Recovery from failure | Dark power → All seems well → Living death → Apparent triumph of dark power → Miraculous escape |

> Pick the plot that matches the emotional arc of your data. The plot is a scaffold, not a straitjacket—adapt stages to your material.

## 6. Plain Language

### Jargon

- Specialised jargon is fine *within* a community that shares the vocabulary.
- Never inflict untranslated jargon on an outside audience.
- Be sparing even with insiders; dense jargon clusters ("leverage synergistic paradigms") reduce readability.

### Acronyms

- Define on first use: "IMS (Instant Messaging System)".
- After that, the acronym alone is fine.

### Business speak

- Plain, precise language builds credibility; pompous phrases erode it.
- Replace clichés with what you actually mean:

| Cliché | Plain alternative |
| --- | --- |
| client-centric | focused on the client's needs |
| key deliverables | the main things we will deliver |
| goal-oriented | with a clear objective |
| manage expectations | be clear about what we can and cannot do |

## 7. Quick-Reference Checklist

Before delivering or publishing a data-based story, verify:

- [ ] I can state the BLOT in one sentence.
- [ ] The audience's Who / What / How questions are answered.
- [ ] Each slide or section has a BLOT-style headline.
- [ ] Information is chunked into ≤ 4 blocks per unit.
- [ ] The narrative follows a recognisable structure (Hero's Journey, BLOT–S–C–R, or one of the Seven Plots).
- [ ] The struggle / complication is not skipped.
- [ ] Jargon and acronyms are defined for the target audience.
- [ ] Business-speak clichés are replaced with plain language.
- [ ] There is a clear call to action or takeaway.
- [ ] I have a three-minute and a one-line version ready.
